The significance of task significance: The overlooked motivational factor. *Journal of Organizational Behavior, 29*(6), 953–967.** Grant’s research shows that perceiving one’s work as having a positive impact ("task significance") is a powerful motivator, linked to increased effort, satisfaction, and reduced burnout.  Interestingly, this effect is *stronger* than the impact of factors like pay or promotion opportunities.

*   **Wrzesniewski, A., Dutton, E. A., & Debebe, G. (2003). Spreading the word: Promoting a positive change in the workplace. *Organization Science, 14*(6), 639–657.** This paper explores “job crafting,” where employees proactively reshape their jobs to better align with their interests and values.  Individuals who engage in job crafting report higher job satisfaction and engagement, even *without* changes to formal roles or rewards.

*   **Ilies, R., & Judge, T. A. (2003). Affective and cognitive predictors of job satisfaction and performance. *Journal of Applied Psychology, 88*(5), 851–865.** This meta-analysis confirms a strong relationship between positive affect (feeling good about your work) and both job satisfaction and performance.  Intrinsic enjoyment of tasks is a major driver of positive affect at work.

*   **Ryan, R. M., & Deci, E. L. (2000). Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being. *American Psychologist, 55*(1), 68–78.**  A comprehensive review reinforcing that environments supporting autonomy, competence, and relatedness enhance intrinsic motivation, which in turn predicts better outcomes. This is also the foundation for understanding why feeling controlled or pressured can be detrimental.



These papers generally support your friends' claim – doing interesting work (aligning with intrinsic motivation) is *very* important, and potentially underrated when considering long-term career satisfaction and even performance. However, remember these studies often involve self-reported data.
